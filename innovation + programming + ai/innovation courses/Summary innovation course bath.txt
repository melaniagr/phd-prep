# Innovation in Healthcare (University of Bath) – Summary

## What innovation is
- Innovation = applying an idea to improve an existing product, process, service or business model (invention + **use**).
- Healthcare innovation includes hard tech (drugs, devices) and soft tech (pathways, protocols, models of care).

## Types and scope
- Radical: major breakthroughs (e.g. new mechanisms, entirely new treatments).
- Incremental: many small improvements to existing technologies (e.g. pacemaker generations).
- Disruptive: simpler, cheaper solutions that open up new markets or replace established ones (e.g. angioplasty vs bypass).
- Position innovation: same service in a new context (e.g. remote monitoring instead of inpatient care).

## Why healthcare innovation is hard
- Complex systems with many stakeholders (patients, clinicians, managers, payers, regulators, industry).
- Risk‑averse, heavily regulated, evidence-based culture; long trials and approval pathways.
- Fragmented economics (separation of payers/providers; primary, secondary, social care).
- Constant trade‑off between better performance, affordability, and value to the whole system.

## Drivers and processes
- Drivers: unmet needs, dissatisfaction with current solutions, technology push, market pull, and serendipity.
- Design thinking / Double Diamond: Discover → Define → Develop → Deliver.
- Tools: 5 Whys, user journeys, personas, spider diagrams, MVP and lean start‑up ideas (used carefully in healthcare).

## Evidence, value and business
- Logic models: map inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impacts to plan and evaluate innovations.
- Value proposition: clear statement of who the innovation is for, which problem it solves, and how it is better (for clinicians, patients, and payers).
- Market sizing: TAM / SAM / SOM; business model canvas to map value, customers, channels, revenues, costs, partners.

## Adoption, spread and culture
- Adoption influenced by: relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, observability.
- Adopters: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards.
- Organisational factors: leadership, receptive culture, absorptive capacity, internal QI capability, and external networks (e.g. AHSNs).
- Quality improvement (PDSA, process mapping, run charts) helps adapt and embed innovations into real pathways.

## Failure, funding and ecosystems
- Many innovations and start‑ups fail; most common reason: “no market need”, even when tech is interesting.
- Other reasons: wrong team, poor UX, no viable business model, running out of cash.
- Funding and support: internal grants, charities, hackathons, crowdfunding, angels, VC, corporate accelerators, open‑innovation portals.
- Innovation ecosystems (universities, health systems, industry, patients) and cross‑sector collaborations are key to development and spread.
