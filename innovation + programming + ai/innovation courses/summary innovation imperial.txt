# Innovation & Diffusion – Summary

## Frugal innovation
- Not just “cheap”: **higher performance at low cost**, designed first for people in resource‑constrained settings.
- Focus on BOP markets (billions living on <\$5/day), with sustainability and social enterprise at the core.
- 10 core competencies: rugged, lightweight, mobile‑enabled, human‑centric, simplified, new distribution models, adapted from existing products, use local resources, green tech, affordable.

## Why Rogers’ model is not enough
- Rogers’ adopter categories (innovators → laggards) are **reductionist**: they over‑focus on individual traits and speed of uptake.
- Being “slow” can be protective when innovations have safety, cost, or sustainability risks—laggards can act as a brake on harmful diffusion.
- Adoption also depends on type of innovation, context, comparative effectiveness, and unintended consequences.

## Evidence, diffusion & soft periphery
- Case studies (LMWH, lap chole, ACT, reusable dialysis filters) show that **evidence strength ≠ adoption speed**.
- Each innovation has a fixed “hard core” (drug, procedure) plus an adaptable “soft periphery” (who uses it, on whom, under what conditions).
- Adoption is more likely when benefits/risks align with interests, values, and power of key actors; compressed learning can create patient risk.

## System & organisational factors
- System readiness: tension for change, innovation–system fit, clear implications, visible champions, time/resources, and ability to evaluate.
- Implementation/routinisation helped by flexible structures, supportive leadership, early staff involvement, training, funding, good communication and feedback, and local adaptation.
- Example contrasts: surgical robots (high‑tech, individualised, teamwork risk) vs Brazil’s Family Health Strategy and CHWs (low‑tech, scaled, community‑embedded).

## Human & social factors
- Diffusion pathways are “messy”: shaped by professional boundaries, identities, and power (e.g., aspirin vs computerised anticoagulation support).
- SHIFT-Evidence framework: act scientifically and pragmatically, embrace complexity, engage and empower people in the system.
- Successful spread depends on relationships (doctors, nurses, managers, patients), not just guidelines.

## Country-of-origin & bias
- External cues (brand, price, **country of origin**) strongly shape perceived quality and willingness to adopt.
- High‑income country sources are implicitly associated with “good research”; low‑income country work is often undervalued even when evidence is identical.
- Ethnocentrism, disidentification, and “not invented here” dynamics affect which innovations and papers get attention.

## IP, funding & translational medicine
- IP tools: patents (20‑year monopoly), design rights, trademarks; complex challenges around new health tech (genomics, CRISPR, data, mHealth).
- Funding options: private equity, venture capital, crowdfunding, business loans—each with specific sources, risks, and trade‑offs.
- Translational research: T1 “bench‑to‑bedside” vs T2 “bedside‑to‑practice/community”; current systems over‑fund T1 relative to T2 implementation work.

## Who innovations are for
- Adoption depends on targeting the right **users and decision‑makers**: frontline clinicians, managers, patients, communities.
- Tools like DoseRight, improvised UBT, and Hemafuse illustrate matching designs to specific settings, roles, and constraints.
