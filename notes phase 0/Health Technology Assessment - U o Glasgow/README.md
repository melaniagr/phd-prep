# Health Technology Assessment (HTA) – Notes (Glasgow)

Notes from the HTA / Health Economics courses, covering principles of priority setting, economic evaluation and global HTA practice.

## What HTA is for

- Health economics applies economic theory and empirical methods to decisions by individuals, providers and governments about health and healthcare.
- HTA is a **multidisciplinary process** using explicit methods to determine the value of a health technology at different points in its lifecycle, to support equitable, efficient, high‑quality health systems.
- HTA informs **priority setting** under resource constraints by comparing options on both outcomes and costs.

## Who does HTA (and at what levels)

- Levels:
  - **Macro** – national policy and funding decisions.
  - **Meso** – guidelines and formularies.
  - **Micro** – local service organisation and efficiency.
- UK examples:
  - NICE (national guidance, mandatory enactment timelines).
  - Scottish Medicines Consortium (SMC), including PACE (patient–clinician engagement) for contentious decisions.
- Producers: governments, HTA agencies (e.g. HEHTA), private sector, universities, clinicians.

## HTA structure and process

- Key questions: What does the technology do? For whom? Do benefits outweigh risks? What is the expected impact if adopted?
- Principles (after Drummond):
  - Clear scope and goals; unbiased and transparent methods; include all relevant technologies and contexts; set priorities based on impact, burden, resources and variation.
  - Methods: incorporate all relevant costs/benefits, consider societal perspective, characterise uncertainty, assess generalisability.
  - Process: engage stakeholders, seek all data, monitor implementation; ensure timely dissemination and explicit links to decision‑making.
- HTA 101 (Goodman): identify topic → define question → search evidence → appraise/synthesise → (if needed) collect new data → economic evaluation → recommendations → disseminate → monitor impact.

## Economic evaluation basics

- Economic evaluation = comparative analysis of alternative actions in terms of **costs and consequences**.
- Costs:
  - Accounting view: fixed vs variable.
  - Health‑economic view: direct, indirect (e.g. productivity loss), intangible (social/emotional).
  - Valued using opportunity cost (tariffs, fees, charges).
- Benefits:
  - Clinical outcomes (e.g. survival, BP, tumour size).
  - Summary measures of health: QALYs (quality × length of life) and DALYs for burden of disease.
- Methods:
  - CMA, CEA, CUA, CBA, CCA depending on how outcomes are valued.
  - Use instruments like EQ‑5D‑5L to capture health‑related quality of life.
- A technology is cost‑effective if its health benefits exceed the opportunity costs of displaced programmes; decisions often use a **cost‑effectiveness threshold**.

## Global HTA & precision medicine

- WHO’s GCEA and WHO‑CHOICE provide generalised cost‑effectiveness analyses to help countries set priorities without running every study locally.
- DALYs and the Global Burden of Disease studies quantify burden and support allocative efficiency; widely used in LMICs.
- HEHTA notes four challenges for economic evaluation of population health and precision medicine: attribution of outcomes, measuring/valuing outcomes, capturing intersectoral costs, and including equity considerations.

## key takeaway

HTA is the bridge between clinical evidence, economics and policy: it asks not just “does this work?” but “is this the best use of limited resources, for this population, given our alternatives?”—crucial for digital health and AI deployment decisions.
